# Guri Teimoso — Respiratory Hospitalization Predictive System

A data-driven pipeline that predicts daily respiratory hospitalizations in **Canoas, RS, Brazil**, by combining public health records with weather and air quality data. Built as the final project for the Data Science course (INF01090) at UFRGS, 2026/1.

**Team:** João Vitor Munhoz, João Vitor Pereira, João Vitor de Souza, Lucas Vargas

---

## 1. Objective

Healthcare systems are frequently overloaded, and without reliable methods to forecast the volume and causes of hospitalizations, institutions struggle to prepare for peak demand periods. This project aims to close that gap by building a data-driven predictive model focused on respiratory-cause hospitalizations in Canoas, RS. The goal is to generate **daily estimates of the number of respiratory hospitalizations**, supporting better resource planning and emergency preparedness for local health institutions.

## 2. Data Sources

The dataset integrates three independent official sources covering the Canoas region:

- **SUS/DATASUS** — daily respiratory hospitalization records from Brazil's public health system. This is the target variable (`internacoes`).
  🔗 [datasus.saude.gov.br](https://datasus.saude.gov.br/informacoes-de-saude-tabnet/)
- **INMET** (Jardim Botânico station, Porto Alegre) — hourly weather data: air temperature, dew point, min/max temperature, relative humidity, wind direction and speed, max wind gust, atmospheric pressure, precipitation, and solar radiation.
  🔗 [portal.inmet.gov.br](https://portal.inmet.gov.br/dadoshistoricos)
- **FEPAM** (Parque Universitário station, Canoas) — hourly air quality measurements: PM10, SO2, NO2, O3, and CO concentrations.
  🔗 [fepam.rs.gov.br](https://www.fepam.rs.gov.br/dados-do-monitoramento)

After merging and cleaning, the final dataset contains **1,521 daily observations**. Some pollution variables had significant missing data (e.g., PM10 at 58%, CO at 44%), which was addressed during preprocessing. The target variable (`internacoes`) was fully observed across all records.

The raw, unprocessed data — before merging and cleaning — is kept in [`dataset-old`](./dataset-old) for reference.

## 6. Methodology

### 6.1 Preprocessing

Irrelevant columns were removed, and the three source datasets were merged on date. Missing values were imputed using **MICE** (Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations), and features were normalized with **MinMaxScaler** and **RobustScaler**.

### 6.2 Feature Engineering

The date column was decomposed into month and day-of-week fields. Binary flags were created for weekends (`fim_de_semana`) and winter months (`inverno`). Lag features (1–3 days) were generated for key variables — minimum temperature, relative humidity, and PM10 — to capture delayed atmospheric effects on health outcomes.

Data was then split via holdout: **2020–2023** for training, spot-checking, and hyperparameter tuning, and **2024** held out for testing.

### 6.3 Spot-Checking

To establish a performance baseline and guide model selection, eight regression algorithms were evaluated using `TimeSeriesSplit` (k=5) cross-validation: Linear Regression, ElasticNet, KNN, Decision Tree, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, HistGB, and LightGBM. Performance was measured via MAE, RMSE, and R².

### 6.4 LSTM Model

Given the sequential nature of the data, an **LSTM** (Long Short-Term Memory) network was selected as the final model. It uses a **7-day input window** to predict the following day's hospitalization count. The model was trained on 2020–2023 data and evaluated on the 2024 test set, with final hyperparameters:

- `hidden_size`: 64
- `num_layers`: 2
- `output_size`: 1 (predicting `internacoes` only)
- `label_width`: 1 (next-day forecast)
- `dropout`: 0.1

## 7. Results

### 7.1 Spot-Checking Baselines

Cross-validated performance of the eight baseline models:

| Metric | Lin. Reg. | ElasticNet | KNN | Dec. Tree | Rand. Forest | Grad. Boost | HistGB | LightGBM |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **R²** | −0.217 | −0.176 | −0.238 | −0.821 | −0.277 | −0.302 | −0.504 | −0.322 |
| **MAE** | 2.309 | 2.259 | 2.329 | 2.846 | 2.361 | 2.384 | 2.556 | 2.420 |
| **RMSE** | 2.986 | 2.937 | 3.023 | 3.659 | 3.062 | 3.089 | 3.313 | 3.118 |

ElasticNet had the best median MAE (2.259) and lowest RMSE (2.937) among classical models. The Decision Tree performed worst across all metrics (R² = −0.821). Every baseline returned a negative R², meaning none consistently beat a simple mean-based prediction — expected given the high day-to-day variability of daily hospitalization counts. This is what motivated moving to an LSTM.

### 7.2 LSTM Performance

On the test set (2024 onward):

| MSE | MAE | RMSE | R² |
|---|---|---|---|
| **5.190** | **1.861** | **2.278** | **0.263** |

The LSTM achieved a positive R² of 0.263 — a substantial improvement over every baseline. Its MAE of 1.861 also beats every spot-checked model, showing the sequential architecture captured temporal dependencies the classical models missed. Residual analysis showed the model performed best on low-to-moderate hospitalization days, with larger errors during peak periods — expected behavior for regression over daily clinical counts. Notably, the final LSTM was conservative in its predictions, staying within a narrow range and rarely predicting extreme high or low hospitalization days.

### 7.3 Interpretability (SHAP)

SHAP analysis was applied to the trained model to quantify each feature's contribution. The most influential variables were SO2 concentration (highest mean absolute SHAP value), day of the week, the weekend indicator, NO2 concentration, precipitation, solar radiation, and max wind gust. Temperature-related features and PM10 lags had moderate importance, while the binary winter indicator had the least impact — suggesting weekly patterns and pollution levels are stronger short-term predictors than seasonal markers.

## 8. Conclusions

This project showed that integrating health, weather, and air quality data into a unified time-series model can meaningfully outperform classical regression approaches for daily respiratory hospitalization forecasting. The LSTM, leveraging its ability to capture temporal dependencies across a 7-day window, outperformed every baseline evaluated in spot-checking.

Feature importance analysis revealed that air quality variables — especially SO2 and NO2 — and temporal patterns (day of week, weekend) are the main factors associated with short-term variation in hospitalizations, giving the model an interpretable public-health meaning.

Still, absolute model performance remains modest. The high intrinsic variability of daily hospitalization counts and the relatively small dataset size are limiting factors. Future work could explore multi-horizon forecasting, ensemble approaches combining the LSTM with classical models, and additional features such as epidemiological surveillance data (e.g., influenza or RSV cases) or more detailed demographic stratifications.
